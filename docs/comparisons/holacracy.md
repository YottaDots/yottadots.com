## What it is

Holacracy is a system for running an organization through explicit roles and
self-organizing circles instead of a management hierarchy. It was developed
by Brian Robertson and is maintained by HolacracyOne. The rules are written
down in the Holacracy Constitution (version 5.0), which the organization
adopts as its source of authority.

## Core idea

Work is split into **roles**, grouped in **circles**. Each circle gets its
purpose from its broader circle and organizes itself. Whoever fills a role has
the authority to take any action that serves the role's purpose, unless a
rule or another role's domain limits it. Structure changes in **governance
meetings**, where proposals pass unless someone raises a valid objection.

## The seven questions

| # | Question | Holacracy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who decides the what? | The broader circle: it gives each circle its purpose and accountabilities. The circle lead sets priorities and strategy within the circle |
| 2 | Do units help shape goals? | Partly: anyone can propose and object in governance (roles and policies), through integrative decision-making. Priorities and strategy are set by the circle lead |
| 3 | What happens to unnamed decisions? | **They stay with the role.** Role leads may take any action that serves their role unless a rule or domain limits it |
| 4 | Can units opt out of shared services? | Not described (as far as we found) |
| 5 | Who decides the how? | The role lead decides how to do the work, but the governance and meeting process is prescribed by the constitution |
| 6 | Practical guidance | Very detailed: the constitution, meeting formats, books and training |
| 7 | Track record | Mixed: used by a range of organizations; Medium dropped it in 2016, and Zappos later moved away from it |

## Strengths

- **Authority is explicit.** Everyone can read who may decide what.
- **Default authority lies with the role,** not with a manager. That is close
  to YottaDots rule 3.
- **Objections are formal:** a valid objection has to be integrated before a
  governance change passes. That is close to YottaDots rule 2.

## Common criticisms

- **Complex.** Medium dropped Holacracy in 2016, saying it got in the way of
  the work.
- **Hard to introduce.** At Zappos, the CEO offered staff a buyout if they did
  not want to work under Holacracy; about 18 percent of the company left within
  ten months. Zappos later quietly moved away from it.
- **All or nothing.** Many organizations now borrow parts of it (role
  catalogues, governance meetings, integrative decision-making) instead of
  adopting the whole constitution.

## When it fits, and when it does not

- **Fits:** organizations that want to replace manager authority with explicit
  rules, and are willing to learn a new, formal way of working.
- **Fits less:** organizations that want to keep managers and add
  safeguards, or that cannot invest in a demanding change.

## How it relates to YottaDots

Holacracy is YottaDots' **closest relative**. Both keep a central purpose,
both give default authority to the people closest to the work, and both make
objections formal.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Compete** | Partly: both answer "who may decide what". |
| **Combine** | A value stream could run internally with Holacracy as its *how*. |
| **Learn** | Integrative decision-making is a worked-out model for rule 2; Holacracy's default authority shows rule 3 can be written down precisely. |
| **Differences** | YottaDots keeps managers and the centre's right to decide the what; it does not prescribe a constitution or meeting process; it applies to value streams rather than individual roles; and it adds opt-out of shared services (rule 4). |
