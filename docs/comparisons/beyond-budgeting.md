## What it is

Beyond Budgeting is a management model that replaces the fixed annual budget
with more adaptive ways to set goals, forecast and allocate resources, and
with more decentralized leadership. It was developed from 1998 by the Beyond
Budgeting Round Table (BBRT), and described by Jeremy Hope and Robin Fraser in
*Beyond Budgeting* (2003). Its proponents call it a management philosophy,
not a recipe.

## Core idea

A traditional budget tries to do three things with one number: set a
**target**, make a **forecast** and **allocate resources**. Those purposes
conflict, and the number becomes political. Beyond Budgeting separates them:
ambitious, relative targets; unbiased rolling forecasts; and resources made
available as needed during the year instead of all at once. Twelve principles,
six on leadership and six on management processes, describe the model.

## The seven questions

| # | Question | Beyond Budgeting |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who decides the what? | Leadership sets purpose and goals. The principles ask for directional, ambitious and relative goals, and warn against fixed and cascaded targets |
| 2 | Do units help shape goals? | Partly: the model favours translating goals locally over cascading them, but a formal right to object is not described (as far as we found) |
| 3 | What happens to unnamed decisions? | Partly: the autonomy principle asks leaders to trust people with freedom to act. A residual-power rule is not described |
| 4 | Can units opt out of shared services? | Not described (as far as we found) |
| 5 | Who decides the how? | The teams, guided by shared values and sound judgement rather than detailed rules. No delivery method is prescribed |
| 6 | Practical guidance | Moderate: principles, books, case stories and a practitioner network. Deliberately not a checklist |
| 7 | Track record | Long but narrow: Handelsbanken has worked without traditional budgets since the early 1970s, and Equinor (formerly Statoil) since 2005. Adoption of the model as a whole is low |

## Strengths

- **It tackles money directly.** It addresses what most organization models
  leave out: budgets, targets, forecasts and resource allocation.
- **Separating the three budget purposes** is a simple, powerful idea that can
  be used on its own.
- **Long-running cases** in large organizations, in banking and energy.
- **Method-agnostic.** It says nothing about how teams deliver.

## Common criticisms

- **Adopted in full by few organizations.** Research finds that adoption of
  the complete model is low, although parts of it are used more widely, often
  under other names. Many organizations prefer to improve their budget rather
  than drop it.
- **Evidence is mostly case studies,** many written by proponents. A 2018
  literature review called the research scattered and asked for more work on
  scaling and implementation.
- **Demanding to introduce.** It touches finance, HR and rewards at the same
  time, and needs long-term commitment from the top.
- **Limited room in the public sector,** where budget rules are often set by
  law.

## When it fits, and when it does not

- **Fits:** organizations where the annual budget causes gaming, late
  decisions or "use it or lose it" spending, and where finance is willing to
  lead the change.
- **Fits less:** where external rules fix the budget cycle, or where leaders
  want the budget mainly as a control instrument.

## How it relates to YottaDots

| | |
|---|---|
| **Compete** | Little. Both decentralize and distrust detailed central control, but they cover different ground. |
| **Combine** | Well. YottaDots says who decides what; Beyond Budgeting says how money and targets follow from that. A YottaDots centre can set relative goals and allocate resources dynamically, in the Beyond Budgeting way. |
| **Learn** | Separating target, forecast and resource allocation. And making resources available as needed, which supports the proposed pattern *Budgets that do not punish saving*. |
