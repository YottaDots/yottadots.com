# Pattern 8: Budgets that do not punish saving

![Visual summary of pattern 8. Left, the use-it-or-lose-it cycle: the budget expires at year end, so it is spent in a rush; unspent money means a smaller budget next year, so spending replaces choices. Right, the YottaDots alternative: part of unspent budget carries over, value streams are judged on outcomes rather than spending, and funding follows priorities, reviewed regularly. Below: saving should never be the expensive choice.](/img/diagrams/pattern-budgets.svg)

## In short

Let value streams carry over part of their unspent budget, and judge them on
outcomes, not on how much they spent. Then nobody has to spend money just to
keep it, and money stops replacing real choices.

## Context

Organizations with yearly budgets that expire at the end of the year,
especially government agencies and large companies with a strict annual
budget cycle.

## Problem

When unspent budget is lost, and next year's budget is based on this year's
spending, saving is punished. People spend the money, often in a rush at the
end of the year, whether or not the spending is the best use. Overspending,
meanwhile, often has few consequences. The result: spending replaces choosing,
and the budget says little about priorities.

This is not only an impression. Research on US federal agencies found that
spending in the last week of the fiscal year was about five times the weekly
average for the rest of the year, and that year-end IT projects were of lower
quality. The one agency that could carry unused IT funds over to the next
year showed no such spike.

## Pattern

1. **Allow carry-over.** Value streams may keep part of their unspent budget
   for the next year, for example up to a set percentage.
2. **Do not cut next year's budget for saving.** Next year's budget follows
   the priorities, not last year's spending.
3. **Judge outcomes, not spending.** Reviews look at what the value stream
   achieved against its goals, not at whether the budget was used up.
4. **Review funding regularly,** for example every quarter, instead of once a
   year. Money can then move to new priorities during the year.
5. **Make overspending visible.** Overspending is discussed in the same review,
   with the same openness.

## Why it works

People respond to incentives. If saving is punished, they spend. If saving is
safe, and outcomes are what counts, the money can go where it does the most
good, or stay unspent. More frequent reviews remove the reason to hold on to
money "just in case", because it can be asked for again when it is needed.

This is close to the ideas of Beyond Budgeting, which questions the fixed
annual budget as the main way to steer an organization.

## Guardrails

- Carry-over has a **limit,** so budgets cannot be hoarded for years.
- Laws and external rules come first. In the public sector, budget rules are
  often set by law or a ministry. Then use whatever room exists, such as an
  internal reserve or multi-year funding, and make the cost of the rules
  visible to those who set them.
- Saving is never the goal in itself. Outcomes are.

## Signals

- Spending at the end of the year is no higher than in other months.
- Value streams report unspent budget without fear.
- Funding moves during the year when priorities change.

## Anti-patterns

- **Carry-over on paper.** It is allowed, but next year's budget is still cut.
- **Hoarding.** Value streams keep money "just in case" because a review is
  far away.
- **Spending as a measure of success.** "We used 100 percent of the budget" is
  reported as an achievement.

## Evidence

- Jeffrey Liebman and Neale Mahoney, "Do Expiring Budgets Lead to Wasteful
  Year-End Spending? Evidence from Federal Procurement", *American Economic
  Review* 107(11), 2017: year-end spending spikes and lower quality, but not
  where carry-over was allowed.
- **[Needs Evidence]** That carry-over and outcome reviews work the same way
  inside a company or a Dutch government agency. No YottaDots cases yet.
