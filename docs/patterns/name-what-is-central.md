# Pattern 1: Name what is central

![Visual summary of pattern 1. On the left, a short published list of what the centre decides: mission and vision, strategy, OKRs and KPIs, and agreed guardrails. On the right, a much larger area for everything else, which stays with the value streams: method, planning, team set-up, tools and anything not on the list. A rule at the bottom: when in doubt, the value stream decides. Adding to the list is a visible decision, never a quiet one.](/img/diagrams/pattern-name-what-is-central.svg)

<small>Status: **proposed (v0.1).** Supports [rule 3](../method/index.md#what-makes-it-a-federation).</small>

## In short

The centre writes down, in one short public list, what it decides. Anything
not on the list belongs to the value streams. When it is unclear, the value
stream decides.

## Context

Any organization where a centre (a board, management team or holding) sets
direction and several value streams deliver. It matters most once the centre
has staff of its own, because staff look for work and decisions to make.

## Problem

Central power grows quietly. No one decides that the centre should approve
tools, hiring or team structure. It happens one reasonable request at a time:
"just check with us first". After a few years, value streams need permission
for things nobody ever gave the centre. Fighting each case is tiring, so most
people stop trying.

## Pattern

1. **Publish the list.** The centre writes down what it decides. Under the
   YottaDots method that is: mission and vision, strategy, OKRs and KPIs, and
   the guardrails that have been agreed. Keep it to one page.
2. **Everything else is the value streams'.** Method, planning, team set-up,
   tools, ways of working, roles, suppliers, meetings, priorities within the
   goals, and anything that is not on the list, including what nobody has
   thought of yet.
3. **When in doubt, the value stream decides.** If it is unclear whether
   something is on the list, the default goes to the value stream until the
   list is changed.
4. **Change the list in the open.** Adding something to the list is a
   decision of its own, announced with a reason. Value streams can object to
   it as they would to a goal (see
   [Objection round on goals](./objection-round-on-goals.md)).
5. **Review it once a year.** Remove items the centre no longer needs to
   decide.

## Why it works

A default changes who has to make the case. Without a list, a value stream
must argue for every freedom. With a list, the centre must argue for every
power it adds. That small reversal makes the slow growth of central power
visible, and makes it cost effort.

This idea is old. It is the principle of subsidiarity: decide at the lowest
level that can decide well. Many federal states, such as the United States,
Germany and Switzerland, write it into their constitution: powers not given
to the federation stay with the states or cantons.
Holacracy uses a similar default at the level of roles (see the
[Holacracy one-pager](../comparisons/holacracy.md)).

## Guardrails

- The list is **public** to everyone in the organization, not only to
  managers.
- The list names **decisions**, not departments. "IT" is not a decision;
  "the approved cloud providers" is.
- The default ("when in doubt, the value stream decides") is itself on the
  list, so it cannot quietly be removed.

## Signals

- People can find the list and answer "who decides this?" without asking a
  manager.
- The list stays short. It grows only through announced decisions.
- Disputes about who decides are settled by the list, not by seniority.

## Anti-patterns

- **The catch-all item.** "And other matters of strategic importance" turns
  the list into a blank cheque.
- **The list nobody reads.** Published once, then forgotten. The old habits
  of asking permission continue.
- **Shadow approval.** The list is short, but central staff still expect to
  be consulted on everything. Watch for "informal" sign-offs.

## Evidence

- Residual powers are a long-standing feature of many federal constitutions, and
  subsidiarity is well established in political and organizational thought.
- **[Needs Evidence]** That a published decision list stops central power
  from growing inside a company. No YottaDots cases yet.
