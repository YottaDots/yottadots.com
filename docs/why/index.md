## In short

Good work starts with clear priorities. In many organizations, the people who
set priorities cannot see the work, and the higher you go, the less clear the
priorities become. Money and hierarchy often make it worse: extra budget or an
extra team replaces a real choice, and new ideas stall at the boss. YottaDots gives the centre one clear job, deciding **what**
matters most, and leaves **how** to the value streams who know the work. The
value streams get a formal voice when goals are set, so the centre hears what
the work really takes.

## The problem

### The centre asks for work it cannot see

Requests, targets and deadlines often come from people who do not see the
work: how much effort it takes, how long it takes, or what else is already
waiting. The result is familiar:

- deadlines that were never realistic;
- too much work started at the same time;
- teams that say yes on paper and fall behind in practice;
- trust that erodes on both sides.

This is not a matter of bad intentions. Distance from the work makes it hard
to judge. People also tend to underestimate how long work will take, a
well-known effect called the planning fallacy. The further away the
decision-maker, the bigger the gap.

### Priorities blur the higher you go

All good work starts with good prioritization. Yet clear priorities are
rarer than most leaders think. In research by Donald Sull, Rebecca Homkes and
Charles Sull among thousands of managers in hundreds of companies, only about
a third of senior managers could name their company's top three priorities.
Only about half of middle managers could name even one of the top five.
Their explanation: leaders' attention is spread over too many things that do
not need to be in their remit.

When the top does not choose, everything becomes important. The choices are
pushed down to teams that lack the overview, or they surface as escalations
and conflicts between departments.

### Money replaces choices

Limited capacity and limited money normally force a choice: if we do this,
we cannot do that. When money is plentiful, or must be spent, that choice
disappears. Two situations we have seen in practice:

- **A high-tech manufacturer with more money than capacity.** Everything was
  important. When a team said no because it had no capacity left, a manager
  hired an extra team, in this case offshore. Over the years this produced
  many separate applications that were hard to maintain, and applications
  that were built but never used. Looking back, one of the developers
  estimated that about 60 percent of their work was never used.
- **A government agency with a use-it-or-lose-it budget.** Overspending had
  few consequences; underspending was punished with a smaller budget the next
  year. So the budget was spent, while real decisions about priorities were
  not made.

In both cases a "no" cannot do its job. It is bought away or spent away.

Both patterns are known beyond these two examples. Product analytics company
Pendo found that about 80 percent of features in the average software
product it studied are rarely or never used. And research on US federal
agencies with expiring budgets found that spending in the last week of the
fiscal year is about five times the weekly average, and that those year-end
projects are of lower quality. The one agency in that study that could carry
unused IT funds over to the next year showed no such spike.

### Ideas need the boss's approval

In a hierarchy, a new idea has to pass the manager. It may stall because the
manager did not think of it (often called the "not invented here" effect), or
because it could weaken the manager's own position. Innovation then depends
on the judgment, and the interests, of one person.

### The problems feed each other

A centre that is busy with the how (approving, checking, steering details)
has less attention for the what. A centre that does not set clear priorities
fills the gap with more requests, more budget and more teams. And when every
idea must pass the boss, the people who see the work stop proposing. All of
this points to the same fix: a centre that does its own job well, makes real
choices, and leaves the rest.

## What YottaDots does differently

YottaDots divides the work in two, with four rules:

| Rule | What it means | Which problem it solves |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The centre decides the what | Mission, strategy and clear, ranked goals (OKRs and KPIs). That is the centre's core job. | Blurred priorities |
| 2. Value streams help shape the what | The centre proposes goals; value streams can object, for example "this does not fit our capacity"; the centre must answer. | Work the centre cannot see |
| 3. Everything not named as central stays with the value streams | The centre has to name what it decides, and cannot quietly take over the how. | A centre busy with the how |
| 4. Value streams may opt out of shared services, unless agreed as a guardrail | Shared services have to earn their users. | Central services that set their own priorities |

**Where the rules are not enough yet.** The four rules do not by themselves
stop money from replacing choices: a centre can answer a "no capacity"
objection by hiring another team. And value streams can object to the what,
but not yet propose to it. We are working on patterns for capacity, usage and
budgets, and on a possible right for value streams to propose. Until then,
these are open problems, not solved ones.

What YottaDots deliberately does **not** do:

- **It does not prescribe a method.** Value streams choose Scrum, Kanban,
  projects, PRINCE2 or something else.
- **It does not prescribe a constitution,** a fixed meeting structure or a
  new set of roles.

## Why now

AI is changing what coordination costs. Much of the work between the centre
and the value streams is collecting and passing on information: status
reports, effort estimates, summaries, the impact of a new request. AI can do
part of that faster and cheaper. That can help the centre see the work it
asks for, and it means fewer layers are needed just to coordinate.

This is a **hypothesis**, not a proven fact. See
[Augment](../method/principles/augment.md).

## For whom

YottaDots is written first for **leaders** who decide how their organization
works: boards, management teams and holding companies. Coaches, consultants
and change leads will find the practical side in the
[Patterns](../patterns/index.md).

It fits when:

- the organization has several value streams, products or business units;
- leaders want clearer priorities and fewer escalations;
- teams already work well locally, but direction and capacity do not match;
- the organization wants to use AI without losing human accountability.

It does **not** fit, or not yet, when:

- the organization is in acute crisis and needs short, central command;
- the top is not willing to name and limit what it decides;
- the organization is small enough for one team to coordinate directly;
- the real problem is elsewhere, for example a missing strategy, or a lack
  of skills or funding;
- the rules that cause the problem are set outside the organization, such as
  budget rules set by law or a ministry. YottaDots can then help only in part.

## What we do not know yet

YottaDots is young. The problems described above are widely recognised, and
there is published research on unclear priorities, unused features and
year-end spending. The two situations described above are practitioner
observations, not studies. That the four rules solve
them better than other approaches has not yet been shown. We will say so
until real cases show it, and we will publish what does not work as well as
what does.
