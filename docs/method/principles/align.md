# Principle 2: Align

![Visual summary of the Align principle, three situations side by side. Left: autonomy without alignment. Value streams move in different directions, which leads to fragmentation. Middle: alignment without a voice. Goals are pushed down and nobody believes them. Right: Align. Shared goals that the value streams helped shape, so everyone moves in one direction, each in its own way. Below: shared direction, local choice.](/img/diagrams/principle-align.svg)

<small>Status: principle of v0.1. Explanation **proposed**.</small>

## In short

The centre proposes shared goals, principles and guardrails. Value streams
help shape them: they can object, make their own proposals and test ideas.
The result is one direction that the people
who do the work understand and support.

## What it means

Federate gives value streams freedom over the how. Align makes sure that
freedom serves a shared purpose. It has two parts:

- **Shared direction.** There is one set of goals (OKRs and KPIs), one set
  of principles and a short list of guardrails that apply to every value
  stream.
- **A voice in that direction.** The centre does not set goals alone. It
  proposes them, value streams can raise reasoned objections and make
  proposals of their own, and the centre must answer both in writing. Value
  streams can also test ideas within a small experiment budget, so an idea
  can prove itself before the centre decides. The centre still decides, but
  not alone.

## Why it matters

- **Freedom without alignment fragments.** Value streams that each optimize
  for themselves end up pulling in different directions, duplicating work
  and confusing customers.
- **Alignment without a voice is not believed.** Goals set without the
  people who must reach them often miss what those people know: limited
  capacity, conflicting targets, or KPIs that reward the wrong behavior.
  People comply on paper and work around the goals in practice.
- **Objections carry knowledge upwards.** A formal right to object is the way
  local knowledge reaches the what. It also makes the centre explain its
  choices, which builds trust even when the answer is no.

## In practice

- Goals are set with an objection round:
  [Objection round on goals](../../patterns/objection-round-on-goals.md).
- Value streams can put their own ideas to the centre:
  [Proposal route](../../patterns/proposal-route.md).
- Value streams can test ideas without approval, within limits:
  [Experiment budget](../../patterns/experiment-budget.md).
- Guardrails are few, owned and reviewed:
  [Guardrails that earn their place](../../patterns/guardrails-that-earn-their-place.md).
- Goals describe outcomes, not methods. How a value stream reaches them is
  its own choice (see [Federate](./federate.md)).

## What it does not mean

- **Not consensus.** Not everyone has to agree. Objections must be answered,
  not necessarily accepted.
- **Not a veto.** Value streams cannot block a goal. The centre decides.
- **Not uniformity.** Being aligned on the what does not mean working the
  same way.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Can every value stream name the shared goals and explain why they matter?
- When did a value stream's objection last change a goal?
- When did a proposal from a value stream last become a goal?
- Do the guardrails still fit on one page?

## Evidence

- Consent-based decision-making with formal objections is used in
  sociocracy and Holacracy, for governance decisions.
- **[Needs Evidence]** That an objection right on company goals leads to
  better or more widely supported goals than ordinary top-down goal setting.
